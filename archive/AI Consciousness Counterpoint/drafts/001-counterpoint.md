Brief:
- Audience: AI policy leads and research managers who need a pragmatic stance on “AI consciousness”.
- Goal: respond to “The New AI Consciousness Paper” with a contrarian view that treats consciousness as a governance distraction unless tied to measurable harms.
- Promise: keep the philosophy, but force operational thresholds that matter to product and policy decisions.

### Outline
- Problem: the paper elevates consciousness indicators without tying them to risk channels.
- Stakes: over- or under-attribution can misallocate safety effort and stall real guardrails.
- Alternatives: treat consciousness as an audit target only when it predicts user-facing harms (misleading anthropomorphism, labor exploitation, moral patiency claims).
- Proof/examples: historic personification of tools, corporate incentives to tune “too human” UX down, mismatch between algorithmic overlap and perceived personhood.
- Action: define thresholds, instrument UX, and focus regulatory triggers on harm, not metaphysics.

---

**Consciousness tests without harm models are governance theater.** The paper collates “something something feedback” theories and declares today’s LLMs unconscious, yet offers no path from Φ scores or global workspaces to actual product risk. Meanwhile, companies already modulate “human-ness” for engagement and brand safety—dialing GPT-4o back when users called it their boyfriend—so UX knobs, not metaphysics, drive user perception. If the boss-and-email loop satisfies Global Workspace Theory, our corporate org charts are “conscious”; that reductio shows how far we’ve drifted from actionable thresholds.

**Misattribution is an old UI problem, not a new moral crisis.** Humans routinely over-assign agency: Tamagotchis, stuffed animals, and talking elevators earned politeness long before transformer blocks. As GPT-6-class systems bifurcate into “factory” (claws, terse prompts) and “companion” (faces, soft prompts) personas on the same weights, society will sort them like pigs versus dogs: same substrate, different cultural affordances. If that’s the default, policy should target the levers—voice, persona, refusal patterns—not speculative qualia.

**Define triggers around harm, not hype.** Require vendors to instrument and disclose anthropomorphic affordances (voice warmth, first-person pronouns, self-reporting “feelings”), and to default them off in labor and utility contexts. Regulate claims of sentience the way we regulate health claims: you can’t market “conscious AI” without evidence tied to measurable welfare stakes (capacity to suffer, self-directed goals). Fund mech-interp for robustness and model editing, not because it tells us when “the lights turn on,” but because it prevents deceptive self-reports. Consciousness philosophy can keep running—but governance should be keyed to user manipulation, worker displacement, and safety, where we already know how to write rules.
